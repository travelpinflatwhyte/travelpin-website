Flying at Christmas

The guide for the busiest week of the year. Where December goes wrong, what to pack, and what you're owed, wherever you're flying.

By Satish, founder of Travelpin. Last checked 18 October 2026, and updated again in early December.

Quietest day to fly

In UK airspace, Christmas Day 2025 had about 2,600 flights. A normal December day had more than 6,100.

Christmas week is the one week of the year when everyone seems to be going somewhere, and the airport is at its least forgiving.

Flights are full, so a cancellation on 23 December can mean waiting days for a seat. Half the world is in winter, with fog and snow at the big hubs. The other half is in peak summer, with storms and record crowds.

This guide is for anyone flying in late December, wherever home is. Find your airport, your route and your rules, and skip the rest.

If you only read one thing

  1. Christmas Day is the quietest day of the year to fly. The Saturday before Christmas is often the busiest: in 2025, both Dubai and Singapore had their busiest day of the season on Saturday 20 December.
  2. Book one ticket, not two. If separate bookings break, nobody owes you a seat, a meal or a hotel.
  3. Check your airport below. Fog, snow and record crowds hit specific airports at specific times. Plan around yours.
  4. Bad weather rarely earns you cash, but it still earns you a new flight or a refund, and in Europe and the UK, meals and a hotel too. Ask before you leave the desk.
  5. Pack the night in your cabin bag. Medication, a charger and a change of clothes. Your checked bag may arrive a day after you do.

Tap an airport for what happened recently and what to do.

  • Delhi India

    What goes wrong
    Dense fog. Aircraft without low-visibility landing clearance are held or diverted first.
    When
    Usually late night to mid-morning, from mid-December into January.
    Recently
    15 December 2025: more than 60 flights cancelled and 250 delayed in one morning.
    What to do
    Choose an arrival after late morning if you can. Check your flight before your family leaves to collect you.

  • London Heathrow United Kingdom

    What goes wrong
    Freezing fog cuts the number of planes that can land.
    When
    Cold, still mornings in winter.
    Recently
    23 January 2023: more than 85 Heathrow flights cancelled in freezing fog.
    What to do
    On a foggy forecast, early flights carry the most risk. Check the night before and in the morning.

  • Amsterdam Schiphol Netherlands

    What goes wrong
    Snow and freezing spells outrun de-icing capacity.
    When
    Any freezing day, all day.
    Recently
    January 2026: KLM cancelled flights and warned its de-icing fluid was running low.
    What to do
    In a cold snap, avoid tight connections here. Leave two hours or more.

  • Frankfurt Germany

    What goes wrong
    Snow and black ice slow runways and de-icing.
    When
    Snow days, December to February.
    Recently
    5 January 2025: 120 of about 1,990 flights cancelled in snow and black ice.
    What to do
    If snow is forecast, look for your airline's free rebooking offer before the day.

  • Munich Germany

    What goes wrong
    Heavy, wet snow can close the airport outright.
    When
    Snow days.
    Recently
    2–5 December 2023: the airport shut, and over 80% of 771 flights were cancelled on the Tuesday.
    What to do
    When heavy snow is forecast, ask your airline about free date changes before the storm arrives.

  • New York JFK, LaGuardia and Newark

    What goes wrong
    Winter storms that hit all three airports at once.
    When
    Storm days, often right around the holidays.
    Recently
    26 December 2025: around 850 flights to and from the three airports cancelled in a post-Christmas snowstorm.
    What to do
    Switching between New York airports rarely helps in a storm. Rebook on a later date instead.

  • Chicago O'Hare United States

    What goes wrong
    Snowstorms and de-icing queues at one of the world's biggest connecting hubs.
    When
    Storm days.
    Recently
    30 November 2025: about 300 flights cancelled and 1,600 delayed after record snowfall.
    What to do
    Book the first flight of the day, and avoid connecting here on a snowy forecast.

  • Toronto Pearson Canada

    What goes wrong
    Winter storms. Airlines often cancel the day before.
    When
    The day before a big forecast storm.
    Recently
    December 2022: WestJet cancelled every Pearson flight for a day before the Christmas weekend.
    What to do
    If a storm is forecast, rebook early. Seats go fast once cancellations start.

  • Dubai United Arab Emirates

    What goes wrong
    Not weather, usually: record crowds.
    When
    The Saturday before Christmas, and the days after.
    Recently
    2025: the busiest day was forecast for Saturday 20 December, with about 303,000 passengers.
    What to do
    The airport advises arriving three hours before departure. Check in online, and say goodbyes at home: in peak periods only passengers can enter the terminals.

  • Doha Qatar

    What goes wrong
    Winter fog around sunrise, which can divert arriving flights to nearby airports.
    When
    Early mornings in winter.
    Recently
    December 2016: fog across Qatar and the UAE diverted flights to Abu Dhabi and Bahrain.
    What to do
    Leave a generous connection if you land around dawn.

  • Singapore Changi Singapore

    What goes wrong
    Crowds rather than weather.
    When
    The weekend before Christmas.
    Recently
    Saturday 20 December 2025: more than 223,000 passengers, the busiest day of the year.
    What to do
    Allow extra time at check-in and security on the weekend before Christmas.

  • Sydney Australia

    What goes wrong
    Summer storms, and knock-on cancellations the next day.
    When
    Storm days in peak summer holidays.
    Recently
    Christmas Day 2023: 23 flights cancelled after Christmas Eve storms and flash flooding.
    What to do
    Don't take the last flight of the day before something you can't miss.

When to fly

Christmas Day is the quietest day of the year. Christmas Eve is the next best. If you can travel on either, you'll find shorter queues and more room to rebook if something goes wrong.

The busiest days depend on where you are:

United States21, 22 and 23 December, then 29 and 30 December on the way back. Both weekends around the holidays will be heavy too.Quieter: 24 and 25 December, 31 December and 1 January, and Friday 18 December.
DubaiThe Saturday before Christmas. In 2025 that was 20 December. In 2026 it falls on 19 December.
SingaporeThe Saturday before Christmas. 20 December 2025 was Changi's busiest day of the whole year.
AustraliaIn 2024, airlines' busiest days were 20 and 22 December, and 27 December after Christmas.

Fly early in the day, with one exception. A morning flight has fewer delays stacked up ahead of it, and if it's cancelled there are more flights left that day to put you on. The exception is a fog-prone airport like Delhi, Heathrow or Doha, where the morning is the risky part.

If your dates are fixed, book now. Seats on the peak days sell out, and the last seats are the most expensive.

One ticket or two

This is the most expensive mistake people make at Christmas, and it's easy to make by accident.

One ticket means every flight is on a single booking, even if different airlines fly them. If your first flight is late and you miss the second, the airline has to get you to your destination. In the UK and EU, your rights to meals, a hotel and compensation are worked out on the whole journey.

Two tickets means separate bookings, often bought to save money. If the first flight is late and you miss the second, the second airline owes you nothing. No rebooking, no meals, no hotel. You buy a new ticket at peak-week prices. Some booking sites sell these as one trip, labelled "self-transfer". Read the small print before you pay.

If you're on two tickets anyway:

  • Leave a long gap. In December, think half a day, not two hours.
  • Check whether you'll need to collect your bag and check in again, and whether you need a visa to pass through the airport to do it.
  • Look for travel insurance that covers missed connections.

Connections in peak week. Even on one ticket, the airline's minimum connection time is a floor, not a plan. In late December, especially through a hub that's prone to snow or fog, aim for at least two hours. If you can see you'll miss it, rebook in the airline's app while you're still on the first plane. The people who rebook first get the seats.

Gifts in your luggage

These rules apply almost everywhere:

  • Don't wrap gifts in your hand luggage. If security needs to look inside, they'll unwrap them. Use gift bags, or wrap when you arrive.
  • If you can spread it, pour it or squeeze it, it's a liquid. Jam, honey, chutney, sauces and soft cheese follow the liquid rules in hand luggage. Put them in your checked bag.
  • Check your departure airport's liquid limit. Some airports with newer scanners allow larger containers, but many still apply 100 ml, and your return airport may not match.
  • Power banks go in hand luggage, and many airlines now ban using them on board. Emirates, Singapore Airlines, Qantas and every airline flying in India are among them, and some ask you to keep them out of the overhead bin. Charge your phone before you board.
  • Weigh your bag at home. Gifts push bags over the limit, and excess weight paid at the airport usually costs far more than adding a bag online.

Then check what you can bring into the country you land in. Customs allowances and food rules are set by each country, and they're stricter than most people expect, especially for food. Here are the official pages for some of the busiest Christmas destinations:

When it goes wrong: what you're owed

The honest part first. Most passenger-rights rules pay cash only when the problem was the airline's fault. Snow and fog usually aren't, so a weather cancellation rarely earns compensation. But it almost always still earns you a new flight or a refund, and in Europe and the UK, meals and a hotel too. Most people walk away without asking.

Which rules apply depends on where you fly from, and sometimes on your airline.

  • European Union

    Flights from the EU, or into the EU on an EU airline

    If it's their fault
    €250, €400 or €600 by distance, for arriving three or more hours late. Long-haul flights three to four hours late get half.
    Even in bad weather
    Meals, a hotel if you're stuck overnight, and a new flight or a refund.
    If they won't pay
    Complain to the national enforcement body in the country where it happened.

  • United Kingdom

    Flights from the UK, or into the UK on a UK or EU airline

    If it's their fault
    £220, £350 or £520 by distance, for arriving three or more hours late. Long-haul flights three to four hours late get £260.
    Even in bad weather
    Meals, a hotel if you're stuck overnight, and a new flight or a refund.
    If they won't pay
    Use your airline's dispute-resolution scheme, or the Civil Aviation Authority.

  • United States

    US airlines, and flights to and from the US

    If it's their fault
    No set cash amount.
    Even in bad weather
    A full cash refund if your flight is cancelled or significantly changed and you choose not to travel: more than three hours late for domestic flights, six for international.
    If they won't pay
    Complain to the US Department of Transportation.

  • Canada

    Flights to, from and within Canada

    If it's their fault
    C$400, C$700 or C$1,000 on large airlines, for arriving three, six or nine hours late.
    Even in bad weather
    A new flight or a refund.
    If they won't pay
    Complain to the Canadian Transportation Agency.

  • India

    Flights within India, and to and from India

    If it's their fault
    Up to ₹5,000, ₹7,500 or ₹10,000 by flight length, if the airline cancels at short notice.
    Even in bad weather
    A new flight or a full refund.
    If they won't pay
    AirSewa, the government's passenger complaints portal.

How to claim, anywhere:

  1. At the airport, ask what you're entitled to before you leave the desk. Meal vouchers and hotel rooms run out fast in Christmas week. If they won't provide them, buy something reasonable yourself and keep every receipt.
  2. Get the reason for the delay in writing, or screenshot the airline's message. It decides whether you're owed cash.
  3. Claim from the airline directly first. Most airlines have a free online form. You don't need to give a claims company a share of what you're owed.

The EU agreed changes to its rules in June 2026, but they aren't expected to apply until 2027. This Christmas, the amounts above still apply.

Three trips, worked out

These are examples with round-number times, to show how the thinking works. Your airline's own cutoffs are what count.

London to Delhi, Monday 21 December

The flight: Heathrow at 9 p.m., landing in Delhi around 11 a.m. the next morning.

Why this one: It lands after Delhi's worst fog hours. A flight landing at 4 a.m. is often cheaper, but it lands right in the fog window, when holds and diversions happen.

Leaving home: Bag drop for long-haul flights often closes an hour before departure, and Heathrow queues in Christmas week are long. Plan to be there three hours early, at 6 p.m. From central London, that means leaving by about 5 p.m.

The family pickup: Immigration and bags in Delhi in December can take an hour or more. Whoever is collecting you should set off once the flight has landed, not when it's due to.

If it breaks: Leaving the UK, you're covered by UK rules. A long delay that's the airline's fault earns up to £520, and a long delay for any reason, weather included, earns meals and a hotel if you're stuck overnight.

Toronto to New York, Wednesday 23 December

The flight: Toronto Pearson at 8 a.m. to LaGuardia.

The catch most people miss: At Toronto you clear US immigration and customs before you board, not after you land. That queue sits between security and your gate, and it's longest in holiday week. Allow at least two and a half to three hours.

Leaving home: Be at Pearson by 5 a.m., so leave home by about 4:15 a.m., depending on where you live.

The weather: If a storm is forecast, Canadian airlines often cancel the day before. Look for your airline's travel waiver and move your flight for free before the rush.

If it breaks: You're on Canadian rules leaving Canada. A delay that's the airline's fault earns up to C$1,000 on a large airline. A storm earns you a new flight or a refund.

Sydney to Singapore, Sunday 27 December

The flight: A morning departure from Sydney.

Why the morning: 27 December is one of the busiest days after Christmas, and summer storms in Sydney can cancel flights and knock on into the next day. A morning flight leaves the rest of the day to rebook you.

Leaving home: Allow at least three hours for an international flight in the peak summer holidays.

Coming home: Australia's food rules are among the strictest anywhere. Declare any food you bring back, including Christmas leftovers and homemade treats. Declaring is free. Not declaring can mean a fine.

If it breaks: Australia has no fixed cash compensation, so your airline's own policy decides what you get. Check it before you fly.
